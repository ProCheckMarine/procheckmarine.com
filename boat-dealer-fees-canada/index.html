Boat Dealer Fees in Canada, Explained by a Dealer

Every boat deal sheet has the boat's price at the top and a stack of smaller lines underneath it. Buyers tend to read those lines one of two ways: as a scam, or as weather — unpleasant but not worth arguing about. Neither is quite right. Most of the lines are real costs that somebody has to pay; a few are margin wearing a costume. I'm a dealer, so read this with that in mind — but the lines below are the same ones you'll see on my desk, and this is honestly what each of them is.

Freight

The boat did not teleport to the lot. Freight is what it cost to ship the boat (and usually the motor and trailer) from the manufacturer to the dealership — trucking, fuel surcharges, the lot. On a deal sheet it typically runs between $500–$2,500 depending on the size of the package and how far it travelled. Custom orders can cost more especially if they're coming from overseas!

Is it negotiable? The cost is real — the dealer paid it or will. What varies is how it's presented: some dealers build it into the advertised price, some show it as a separate line. A separate line isn't automatically padding. The question to ask isn't "why is there freight?" — it's whether the freight figure is the actual freight or a round number that grew in the telling. A dealer who can show you the freight invoice is telling you something about how the rest of the sheet will go.

PDI and rigging

PDI is the pre-delivery inspection: the boat or motor comes out of shrink wrap, gets unboxed, assembled where needed, and checked over. Rigging is the physical work of marrying boat, motor and trailer into a working package — mounting and connecting the outboard, rigging the controls and gauges, installing the battery, fitting the prop, setting up the trailer, then fuel, a systems check and usually a run so the first start isn't yours. That is technician hours, and technician hours are the most expensive hours in the building. Expect $95 for portable outboard to $995 for more complex packages.

This is the fee buyers most often try to delete and the one I'd argue hardest to keep — done properly. A rigged, tested boat that starts, steers, charges and doesn't leak is worth more than a discount on a boat that was waved out the gate. Sometimes it is a requirement for the manufacturer's warranty. What you're entitled to ask: what exactly does your PDI include? A good answer is a checklist, not a shrug.

Documentation / admin fee

The doc fee covers the paperwork of the deal: the sales documents, the lien and title checks on your trade, registration handling, financing paperwork if you're borrowing, and the administrative staff time behind all of it. It typically runs $200–$1,000.

Honest answer on this one: it's part cost recovery and part margin, and every dealer knows it. It's also almost always a flat, non-negotiable line — the same for every customer, because charging different doc fees to different buyers on the same day is a fairness and compliance problem. Where you have leverage is the total deal, not this line. If a dealer "removes" the doc fee, watch the number it reappears inside.

Licence and registration handling

Somebody has to transfer or issue the Pleasure Craft Licence, handle the trailer registration, and process the tax forms. Deal sheets may show a line for this work in the range of $100 to $500, or fold it into the doc fee above. Either way it's small, it's mostly labour, and the test is the same: ask what it covers, and if the answer duplicates the doc fee's answer, ask why it's twice.

Levies that ride along with the trailer

Because a boat package includes a trailer, some of the levies you associate with buying a vehicle can show up attached to it — tire levies are the usual one — $7 per tire in BC. These are pass-through charges, not dealer inventions; the amount is set outside the dealership and the dealer collects it the way they collect tax. If a levy line doesn't correspond to anything on the package — count the tires, including the spare, and see if the arithmetic agrees.

So which lines are negotiable?

Roughly, in order:

  • Pure margin lines — anything a dealer can't explain in one sentence, or that duplicates another line. Negotiable to zero, because they shouldn't exist.
  • The boat's selling price itself — this is where the real negotiation lives, not in the fee stack.
  • Freight and PDI — real costs. You can negotiate the deal down by their amount, but expect the lines to stay on the sheet; a dealer who deletes the line and keeps the total has given you typography, not money.
  • The doc fee — effectively fixed. Treat it as part of the total and negotiate the total.

The one number that settles all of this

Ask for the out-the-door price: the total you will actually pay, including the boat, every fee and levy, and taxes, in writing, before you agree to anything. A dealer working clean will give it to you — it's how they have to think about the deal anyway once financing and taxes are calculated. Then compare dealers on that number alone, for the same boat, motor, trailer and equipment list. Two quotes more than 5% apart on the same package usually differ in the fee stack and the equipment list, not in anyone's generosity.

And when a price looks too good, check the sheet for the reverse trick: a low advertised number with the freight, rigging and fees waiting underneath it. The advertised price is marketing. The out-the-door number is the deal.
