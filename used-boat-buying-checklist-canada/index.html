The Used Boat Buying Checklist (Canada Edition)

This is the short version of the checklist I use at the dealership, written for a Canadian buyer standing at a stranger's dock or driveway with a phone in hand. It will catch most of the expensive problems. It is not the whole job — at the end I'll tell you honestly what the full paid version adds, and you can decide whether you need it.

Work in order. Paperwork first, because the prettiest boat in BC is a bad buy if the seller can't cleanly sell it.

1. Paperwork — before you fall for the boat

  • Ask for the pleasure craft licence (PCL) or registration documents and check the seller's name against their photo ID. The person selling should be the person on the paperwork, or able to explain exactly why not.
  • Know what a licence is — and isn't. A PCL identifies the boat; it is not proof of ownership. Look for a bill of sale chain or other proof the seller owns what they're selling.
  • Get the identifiers: the hull identification number (HIN, usually on the upper starboard corner of the transom), the outboard's serial number, and the trailer's VIN. Photograph all three.
  • Check for liens. Run the searches before money moves — a lien on the boat, the motor or the trailer follows the property, not the person who borrowed against it. (Our full guides walk through this in detail: Buying a Used Boat in BC: Paperwork, Licence Transfer & PST and How to Check a Used Boat for Liens in Canada.)
  • Ask about tax plainly. In BC, expect PST on a used boat purchase — who collects it and how much depends on whether you're buying privately or from a dealer. Get the answer before you agree on a price, not after.
  • Agree on a proper bill of sale covering boat, motor and trailer as separate items with their identifiers and the agreed prices.

2. Hull and deck

  • Walk the hull slowly. Look for cracks around the transom and through-hull fittings, blisters, repairs that don't match, and a waterline that suggests she sits unevenly.
  • Press and walk the deck and sole — soft, spongy spots mean water in the core.
  • Open every hatch and locker. You're looking for standing water, heavy mildew, and wiring that looks like a science project.
  • Check the bilge: oil, fuel smell, and how much water is sitting there — and ask when the bilge was last dry.

3. The transom — the check people skip

  • With the outboard tilted up, push and pull on it firmly. The transom should not flex.
  • Look for cracks at the corners, a gap or bulge around the mounting bolts, and sealant smeared over problems.
  • A soft or flexing transom on a boat you otherwise love is a major repair conversation, not a cosmetic one.

4. The outboard

  • Insist on a cold start. An engine already warm when you arrive has been warmed up for a reason — good or bad. Cold starting is where weak batteries, tired starters and fuel problems show up.
  • Watch for the telltale stream once it's running, and listen: even idle, no knocking, no grinding.
  • Check the engine oil (four-stroke) — level, colour, and no milky emulsion under the filler cap.
  • Look at the gear oil if the seller will allow the bottom drain screw to be cracked: milky means water intrusion.
  • Note the dash hour reading — then treat it as a claim, not a fact. Ask about service records, and on a modern fuel-injected engine, whether a diagnostic printout is available. (Our guide Boat Engine Hours: Why the Dash Gauge Can Lie explains where the real number lives.)
  • For an engine worth real money, a compression test — done by a dealer or shop, not guessed at — belongs in the deal. Even readings across cylinders are what you're hoping to see. (Our guide Outboard Compression Test, in Plain Language: What It Tells a Buyer explains what the readings actually mean, and what to check in the lower unit while you're at it.)

5. The trailer

  • Check the VIN plate against the paperwork, and confirm the trailer is registered to the seller — in BC the trailer is its own vehicle with its own registration and its own tax treatment.
  • Look at tires for sidewall cracking and age, not just tread. Spin the wheels and listen for rough bearings.
  • Check bunks and rollers, the winch strap, lights, and the coupler for wear and rust.

6. The sea trial

  • Never skip it on a boat that floats. Problems that hide at idle confess at speed.
  • Cold start again if you can. Note how she planes, whether she pulls to one side, steering play, and any vibration through the rev range.
  • Run long enough to reach full operating temperature, then check again for leaks, smells and new noises.
  • Test everything electrical you can reach: pumps, lights, trim and tilt, gauges, electronics.

Walk-away signals

Any one of these should slow you right down — most should end the viewing:

  • Paperwork that doesn't match the seller, the boat, or the numbers on the hull
  • A seller who refuses a cold start, a sea trial, or an independent inspection
  • A flexing transom, milky gear oil brushed off as "normal," or hours that don't match the records
  • A price far below similar boats, with a reason that keeps changing
