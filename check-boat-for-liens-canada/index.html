How to Check a Used Boat for Liens in Canada, Province by Province

A used boat can carry somebody else's debt. If the seller financed the boat, a lender may hold a registered claim against it — and if you buy without checking, that claim can follow the boat to you. The check itself is cheap and takes minutes to arrange. The part nobody explains is that there is no Canada-wide lien registry for boats. Every province and territory runs its own personal property registry, a search covers only the jurisdiction you search, and Quebec doesn't use the same system at all. A boat that has lived in two provinces needs a search in both.

The four identifiers you need first

Before you can search anything, get these from the seller — this set is standard practice among buyers, surveyors and dealers, and each one maps to a real search category in the registries:

  1. The HIN — the hull identification (serial) number. Boats are "serial numbered goods," and this is the number a serial search runs on.
  2. The outboard motor's serial number. The motor is searched separately from the hull. A loan taken on the motor alone won't necessarily show up on a hull search.
  3. The trailer's VIN. Trailers are serial numbered goods in every system — a third, separate search.
  4. The seller's exact legal name. Some claims are registered against the person generally, not against the boat's serial number, so a serial-only search can miss them. In Ontario, individual debtors are indexed by name and date of birth — a search with the wrong birth date can fail to disclose registrations that exist.

Write all four on the bill of sale. You'll need them again.

British Columbia

BC's Personal Property Registry (PPR) covers boats, outboard motors and trailers by serial number, plus debtor-name searches. The catch: the public can't self-serve online. BC Registries reserves direct online access for professionals with premium accounts. As an ordinary buyer you have three routes:

  • In person at a Service BC location, where staff run the search
  • By mail to BC Registry Services, with results returned by mail
  • Through a third-party service provider, who may add their own fee

A staff-conducted search costs $10. (Account holders searching through the system themselves pay $7 plus a $1.50 operator fee — that's the professional route, not yours.) Run both a serial search on each of the hull, motor and trailer, and a name search on the seller.

Alberta

Alberta's Personal Property Registry is delivered through licensed registry agents — there's no government self-serve portal for the public. You fill out a search request and submit it at a registry agent office; some agents also sell searches online. Serial searches expressly cover boats and outboard motors, and name searches are available. Alberta doesn't publish a single search fee — agents charge the government fee plus their own service charge, so the price depends on the agent you use.

Saskatchewan

The most buyer-friendly system in the country. Saskatchewan's registry (run by ISC) is self-serve online, 24/7, with a free online account you can create with an email address. A search by serial number, debtor name or registration number costs $11. Staff-assisted searches add a $10 fee.

Manitoba

Manitoba's Personal Property Registry is operated by Teranet Manitoba and is searchable online by name, serial number or registration number. The search fee is set by regulation — a base of $16.30 adjusted annually by formula — so expect roughly $16–$17 and confirm the current figure on Teranet Manitoba's fee schedule when you search.

Ontario

Ontario's PPSR is searched online through ServiceOntario's "Access Now," and one search does double duty: the same registry records both security interests and Repair and Storage Liens Act claims, so a marina or repair bill claim can show up alongside a lender's. A search costs $8. You can search by serial number, by business name, or by individual name — for an individual, the reliable "specific" search needs the seller's first name, surname and date of birth, for the indexing reason explained above.

Quebec — the trap

Quebec has no PPSA. Rights in movable property are published in the RDPRM, and here's the catch that catches boat buyers: a search by serial number exists only for road vehicles. A boat is not a road vehicle, so the hull and the outboard can only be searched by the owner's name — $13 per name for a given date of birth. (The trailer is a road vehicle, so it can be searched by VIN for $5.) If you go in expecting to type in a HIN the way you would in another province, you'll come away thinking there's no way to check. There is — it's just a name search.

Atlantic Canada and the territories — one shared system

New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island, Newfoundland and Labrador, Yukon, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut each have their own registry, but all seven run on the same platform: ACOL PPRS. For a boat buyer the useful door is Lien Check — no account needed, just a credit card — which searches serial-numbered goods, with "boat" and "outboard motor" expressly listed. Debtor-name searches need a full ACOL client account.

Fees per search: Nova Scotia $8.75; Nunavut $5; New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island, Newfoundland and Labrador, Yukon and the Northwest Territories $10 each.

The federal layer: registered vessels

Everything above is provincial. There's one more place a claim can live. Most pleasure craft are only licensed, and there's no federal lien record for them. But if the boat you're buying is a registered vessel — in the Canadian Register of Vessels — a marine mortgage can be registered against it federally, and it will not appear in a provincial search (just as a provincial registration won't appear federally). For a registered vessel, order a transcript of register from Transport Canada: $75 certified, $20 uncertified. It shows the registered ownership history and any registered mortgages. Registered boat? Check both systems.

Reading the result — and what to do with a hit

A clean result means no registrations were found against the criteria you searched, in that registry, at that moment. It is a snapshot, not a guarantee: it won't show claims registered in another province, under a different name spelling, or unregistered claims — a repairer who still physically holds the boat, for example, may have a claim no registry shows. And run the search as close to the day money changes hands as you can; registrations can be filed any day.

If a search comes back with a hit, that is not automatically a dead deal — but the deal stops until it's sorted. A result names the secured party but does not tell you how much is still owed; that figure comes from the secured party. The normal, safe sequence: the seller gets a payout statement from their lender, the debt is discharged as part of the sale (ideally with the lender paid directly out of the purchase funds), and you get evidence of the discharge before or as the boat changes hands. What you don't do is hand over full price on a promise that the loan will be paid off next week.

Where this fits in a purchase

A lien search is one step in a proper used-boat purchase, alongside the physical inspection, the paperwork and the sea trial. No. 01, the Used Boat & Outboard Buyer Checklist, puts the whole sequence in order — including these searches, the identifiers to collect, and what to do with the results — the way it's done from the sales desk side of the counter.

— Michael Austin, ProCheck — marine sales, Port Coquitlam, BC
