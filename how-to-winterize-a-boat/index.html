How to Winterize a Boat (and Outboard) in Canada

Winterizing is not one job. It is a sequence of small jobs, and the order matters — a couple of them only work if they happen while the engine can still be run, and one of them (the gear oil) is really an inspection disguised as a service. Done in the fall, this routine is a weekend morning. Skipped, it becomes the spring failures every service desk in the country can recite from memory.

Here is the order we walk owners through, and why each step sits where it does.

1. Start with the fuel — while you can still run the engine

Modern gasoline does not store well. Over a winter, untreated fuel absorbs moisture, separates, and leaves deposits through the fuel system. The fix has to happen before lay-up, not after: add a fuel stabilizer at the dosage on the bottle, top up the tank, and then run the engine long enough for treated fuel to reach every part of the system — carburettors or injectors included. Treated fuel sitting in the tank while untreated fuel sits in the engine is the most common half-job we see.

2. Run it, flush it, warm it

With the boat on the trailer and a water supply connected (or at the ramp before you haul out), run the engine to operating temperature. This does three things: it circulates the treated fuel, it flushes raw water out of the cooling passages, and it warms the oils so they drain properly in the next steps. Saltwater boats benefit most from the flush — salt left in the passages all winter keeps corroding.

3. Change the gear oil — and actually look at it

This is the inspection disguised as a service. Drain the lower-unit gear oil and read what comes out:

  • Clean, dark oil — normal. Refill with fresh oil of the type and grade your owner's manual specifies, and you're done.
  • Milky or coffee-coloured oil — water is getting in, which means a seal is failing. Caught in the fall, that's a seal job. Left all winter, that water sits against gears and bearings for months, and can freeze and crack the gearcase.
  • Metal filings on the drain magnet — a small amount of fine fuzz is normal wear; chunks or flakes are a conversation to have with a technician before spring, not during it.

Changing gear oil in the fall instead of the spring is the single highest-value habit on this page. It costs little, and it's the only step that tells you something you didn't know.

4. Engine oil and filter (four-strokes)

On a four-stroke outboard or inboard, change the engine oil and filter as part of lay-up. Used oil holds combustion acids and moisture; leaving it in the crankcase all winter means leaving it in contact with bearings and internal surfaces for months. Fresh oil in the fall is storage protection, and it means one less job between you and the first spring launch. Use the oil and capacity in your manual — there is no universal answer, and anyone who gives you one without asking which engine you have is guessing.

5. Fog it, or store it treated — then stop turning it over

For engines going into storage, fogging oil coats the internal surfaces against corrosion. Follow the procedure for your engine type — carburetted, EFI and direct-injection engines are not all fogged the same way, and your manual is the authority. Once it's stored, resist the urge to start it "just to keep it alive" mid-winter: a short run that never reaches full temperature adds moisture and condensation without burning it off. A properly stored engine is better left asleep.

6. Drain every water system

Any water left in the boat will freeze, and ice expands with enough force to crack engine blocks, manifolds, pumps and plumbing. Work through the list:

  • Inboards and sterndrives: drain the block and manifolds at the drain plugs your manual identifies — this is the step behind the most expensive sentence in boating, "the block cracked over the winter."
  • Livewells, washdown pumps, freshwater tanks and lines: drain and, where the manufacturer allows, run non-toxic plumbing antifreeze through them.
  • Bilge: pump it dry and sponge out what the pump can't reach. A frozen bilge is a cracked through-hull fitting waiting for launch day.
  • Outboards: store tilted fully down so the lower unit drains completely and rainwater can't pool in it.

7. Battery out, charged, stored off the floor

Disconnect the battery, remove it, clean the terminals, charge it fully, and store it somewhere cool and dry, off bare concrete. Check it and top up the charge once or twice over the winter. A battery left in the boat, discharged, will usually be a dead battery by spring — sulfation doesn't reverse — and it always seems to be discovered at the launch ramp.

8. The trailer is part of the boat

Winterizing stops at the waterline for most owners, but the trailer sits outside in the same weather. Check the tire pressures and condition, look at the bearing protectors, work the winch and coupler, and test the lights before storage rather than after. If the boat is stored on the trailer, take weight off the tires where you can and keep the tongue supported. Spring-you, backing down the ramp, sends thanks.

9. Cover it like you mean it

A cover should shed water, not hold it: supported so it can't pool, vented so the interior can breathe, and snug enough not to flap itself — and the gelcoat — apart in a February wind. Mildew is a ventilation problem, not a cleanliness problem; a sealed, damp boat comes out of storage smelling like it. Leave lockers open, cushions up or out, and nothing organic behind.

What spring looks like when fall was done right

Commissioning a properly winterized boat is short: battery in, plugs and hoses checked, a run on the hose or at the dock before the first real trip, and a look at every system before it's needed. That's the whole promise of the fall routine — an hour of prevention for every day of spring repair it replaces.
